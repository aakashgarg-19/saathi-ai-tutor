# Microorganisms: Friend and Foe

## What are Microorganisms
There are many living organisms around us that are too small to be seen with the naked eye. They are called microorganisms or microbes. We can see them only with the help of a magnifying glass or a microscope. Microorganisms are found everywhere: in air, water, soil, inside the bodies of plants and animals, and even in very hot springs and icy places.

Microorganisms are classified into four major groups: bacteria, fungi, protozoa and algae. Viruses are also microscopic, but they are different from other microorganisms because they reproduce only inside the cells of a host organism such as a bacterium, plant or animal. Some microorganisms, like many bacteria, are single-celled, while others, such as some fungi and algae, are multicellular.

## Useful Microorganisms in Food
Microorganisms have been used by people for thousands of years. The bacterium Lactobacillus helps in turning milk into curd. When a little curd is added to warm milk, the bacteria multiply and convert the milk into curd. Bacteria are also used in making cheese, and microorganisms help in the fermentation of idli and dosa batter, making it rise and giving it a sour taste.

Yeast, a fungus, is used in baking bread, cakes and pastries. Yeast reproduces rapidly and produces carbon dioxide during respiration. Bubbles of this gas fill the dough and increase its volume, which makes bread soft and spongy.

## Fermentation
Yeast is also used on a large scale to produce alcohol and vinegar. Yeast is grown on natural sugars present in grains such as barley, wheat and rice, and in crushed fruit juices. The process in which sugar is converted into alcohol by yeast is called fermentation.

Louis Pasteur discovered fermentation in 1857. Fermentation is an important process used in industries to make many useful products, and it shows how microorganisms can be put to work for human needs.

## Medicines and Vaccines
When you fall ill, a doctor may give you antibiotics. Antibiotics are medicines that kill or stop the growth of disease-causing microorganisms. They are made from certain bacteria and fungi. Streptomycin, tetracycline and erythromycin are commonly used antibiotics. Penicillin was discovered by Alexander Fleming in 1929 from a mould.

Antibiotics must be taken only on the advice of a qualified doctor, and the full course must be completed. Taking antibiotics unnecessarily or stopping them midway can make bacteria resistant, so the medicine will not work well in the future. Antibiotics do not work against colds and flu because these are caused by viruses.

When a disease-causing microbe enters our body, the body produces antibodies to fight it. The body also remembers how to fight the microbe if it enters again. A vaccine works on this principle: dead or weakened microbes are introduced into a healthy body, which then produces antibodies and becomes protected from the disease. Diseases like cholera, tuberculosis, smallpox and hepatitis can be prevented by vaccination. Edward Jenner discovered the vaccine for smallpox in 1798. India's pulse polio programme is a well-known example of a large vaccination drive.

## Microorganisms and Soil Fertility
Some bacteria and blue-green algae are able to fix nitrogen from the atmosphere into the soil, increasing its fertility. These microbes are called biological nitrogen fixers. Rhizobium, which lives in the root nodules of leguminous plants like beans and peas, is an important example.

Microorganisms also clean the environment. They decompose dead plants, animals and organic waste such as vegetable peels into simple substances. These substances return to the soil as nutrients and are used again by plants. This is the idea behind making compost from kitchen waste.

## Harmful Microorganisms and Diseases
Some microorganisms cause diseases in humans, plants and animals. Disease-causing microorganisms are called pathogens. Diseases that spread from an infected person to a healthy person through air, water, food or physical contact are called communicable diseases. Cholera, typhoid, tuberculosis, chickenpox and the common cold are examples.

Some insects and animals act as carriers of disease-causing microbes. The housefly carries germs from garbage and animal waste and deposits them on uncovered food. The female Anopheles mosquito carries the parasite that causes malaria, and the female Aedes mosquito carries the dengue virus. Stagnant water during the monsoon is a breeding ground for mosquitoes, so we should not let water collect in coolers, tyres and flower pots.

Microorganisms also cause diseases in plants, such as citrus canker, rust of wheat and yellow vein mosaic of bhindi, reducing crop yield. In animals, anthrax is a dangerous disease caused by a bacterium, and foot and mouth disease of cattle is caused by a virus.

## Food Poisoning and Food Preservation
Some microorganisms grow on our food and produce toxic substances, making the food poisonous. Eating such spoilt food can cause serious illness called food poisoning. To prevent this, we preserve food.

Common methods of food preservation include: using salt, as in pickles, raw mangoes and fish; using sugar, as in jams and jellies, because sugar reduces moisture and stops bacterial growth; using oil and vinegar, which prevent the growth of microbes in pickles; heating and cooling, such as boiling milk and storing food in a refrigerator, because low temperature slows the growth of microbes; and pasteurisation, in which milk is heated to about 70 degrees Celsius for 15 to 30 seconds and then suddenly chilled. Chemical preservatives such as sodium benzoate and sodium metabisulphite are also used in packaged foods. Airtight packaging is used to keep dry fruits and snacks fresh.
