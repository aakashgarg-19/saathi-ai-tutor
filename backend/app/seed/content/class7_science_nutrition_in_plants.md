# Nutrition in Plants

## What is Nutrition
All living things need food. Food gives energy for work, helps the body grow, and repairs damaged parts. The process of taking in food and using it is called nutrition. Carbohydrates, proteins, fats, vitamins and minerals are the components of food, and they are called nutrients.

Animals, including humans, cannot make their own food. They depend on plants directly or indirectly. Organisms that make their own food from simple substances are called autotrophs, and this mode of nutrition is autotrophic nutrition. Organisms that take food made by others are called heterotrophs, and their mode of nutrition is heterotrophic nutrition. Most green plants are autotrophs, while animals, fungi and most bacteria are heterotrophs.

## Photosynthesis
Green plants make their food by a process called photosynthesis. The word comes from "photo", meaning light, and "synthesis", meaning putting together. During photosynthesis, plants use carbon dioxide from the air and water from the soil to make carbohydrates in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll. Oxygen is released as a by-product.

The process can be written as: carbon dioxide + water, in the presence of sunlight and chlorophyll, gives carbohydrate + oxygen. The carbohydrate first formed is a simple sugar, glucose, which is later changed into starch and stored. The presence of starch in a leaf is a sign that photosynthesis has taken place. We can test for starch by adding a few drops of iodine solution to a leaf that has been boiled in alcohol; the leaf turns blue-black.

Photosynthesis is the main source of food for almost all living things on Earth. It also keeps adding oxygen to the air, which animals need for breathing. Without photosynthesis, life on Earth would not be possible.

## Chlorophyll and Leaves
Leaves are called the food factories of a plant. Leaves contain a green pigment called chlorophyll, which captures the energy of sunlight. This energy is used to make food from carbon dioxide and water. Chlorophyll is present inside tiny structures called chloroplasts in the leaf cells.

Some leaves are red, brown or violet in colour. These leaves also contain chlorophyll, but the large amount of red or other pigments hides the green colour. So these leaves can also carry out photosynthesis. Parts of a plant other than leaves, such as green stems and green branches, can also make food because they also contain chlorophyll. Even in deserts, cactus plants carry out photosynthesis in their green stems because their leaves are reduced to spines to save water.

## Stomata and Transport of Water
The surface of a leaf has tiny pores called stomata. Each pore is surrounded by two guard cells that open and close it. Carbon dioxide from the air enters the leaf through the stomata, and oxygen produced during photosynthesis leaves through them. Water vapour also escapes through stomata.

Water and minerals are absorbed from the soil by the roots. They are carried up to the leaves through special tube-like vessels in the stem called xylem. Food made in the leaves is carried to all other parts of the plant through another set of vessels called phloem. Thus the roots, stem and leaves work together as one system to help the plant make and distribute food.

## Making Proteins and Fats
Plants also make proteins and fats, not just carbohydrates. Proteins contain nitrogen. Air has plenty of nitrogen gas, but plants cannot use it directly. Certain bacteria present in the soil convert nitrogen gas into a usable form and release it into the soil. Plants absorb these nitrogen compounds through their roots along with water and other minerals.

Farmers add manure and fertilisers rich in nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium to the soil to replace the nutrients used up by crops. Using carbohydrates made during photosynthesis together with nitrogen and other minerals from the soil, plants make proteins and fats.

## Parasitic Plants
Some plants do not have chlorophyll and cannot make their own food. They depend on other plants for food. A plant that lives on another plant and takes food from it is called a parasite, and the plant on which it lives is called the host. Cuscuta, also called amarbel, is a common parasitic plant. It has yellow, thread-like stems that twine around the host plant. Cuscuta takes food from the host through special structures and harms the host by depriving it of nutrients.

## Insectivorous Plants
A few plants can trap and digest insects. These are called insectivorous plants. The pitcher plant is a well-known example. Its leaf is modified into a pitcher-like structure with a lid at the top. The inside of the pitcher has hair that point downward. When an insect lands inside, it gets trapped among the hair and cannot climb out. It is then digested by juices produced in the pitcher.

Insectivorous plants are green and can make food by photosynthesis. They usually grow in soil that is poor in nitrogen, so they get extra nitrogen by digesting insects. The Venus flytrap and the sundew are other examples.

## Saprotrophs
Have you seen white or black cotton-like growth on stale bread or on pickles left open during the monsoon? These are fungi. Fungi get nutrition from dead and decaying matter. This mode of nutrition, in which organisms take in nutrients in solution form from dead and decaying matter, is called saprotrophic nutrition, and such organisms are called saprotrophs. Fungi release digestive juices on the dead matter, convert it into a solution, and then absorb the nutrients. Mushrooms are also fungi.

Saprotrophs are very useful in nature. By decomposing dead plants and animals, they return nutrients to the soil, where they can be used again by plants. Fungal spores are present in the air and grow quickly in warm and humid conditions, which is why food spoils faster in the rainy season.

## Symbiosis
Some organisms live together and share both shelter and nutrients. Such a relationship, in which both partners benefit, is called symbiosis. Lichens are a good example. In a lichen, a chlorophyll-containing partner called an alga lives together with a fungus. The fungus provides shelter, water and minerals to the alga, and the alga provides food made by photosynthesis to the fungus.

Another important example is found in the roots of pulse crops such as gram, peas and moong. Their roots have small swellings called root nodules, which contain a bacterium named Rhizobium. Rhizobium converts atmospheric nitrogen into a form plants can use, and in return the plant provides food and shelter to the bacterium. This is why farmers grow pulses in rotation with other crops, as it naturally restores nitrogen to the soil.
